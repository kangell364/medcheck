## 1. The fact that defines this market

**Texas does not require pre-licensing education for a General Lines agent
licence.** TDI does not mandate or approve agent pre-licensing courses; its
approval rules cover continuing education and *adjuster* pre-licensing only.
The one exception is the temporary licence, which requires 40 hours within 14
days of application.

This is the single most important thing about the market you are entering.

In states that mandate pre-licensing, vendors sell **compliance** — the
candidate must buy something, and the cheapest approved option often wins. In
Texas, every purchase is discretionary. A candidate could legally walk into the
exam having read nothing.

Two consequences:

- **You are not competing on price against a mandate.** You are competing
  against "I'll just read a book" and "I'll wing it." The product has to be
  visibly worth paying for.
- **Everyone therefore sells outcome, not content.** Which is why nearly every
  competitor leads with a pass-rate claim and a guarantee — that is the only
  axis left.

## 2. The number that should shape the product

Pearson VUE publishes Texas pass rates monthly. For **General Lines — Property
& Casualty (English)**:

| Month | First-time takers | Repeat takers |
| ----- | ----------------- | ------------- |
| June 2024 | 60% | 36% |
| December 2024 | 60% | 38% |
| March 2025 | 60% | 37% |
| April 2025 | 57% | 34% |
| January 2026 | 61% | 36% |

Two things stand out.

**About 4 in 10 fail on their first attempt.** The market of people who need
help is large, motivated, and continuously replenished.

**Repeat takers do dramatically *worse* — roughly 36% against 60%.** This is
the interesting one, and it is counterintuitive: someone who has already seen
the exam, knows the format, and has every incentive to prepare, performs *worse*
than a newcomer.

Part of that is selection — candidates who failed once are, on average, weaker
or less prepared. But the gap is far too large for selection alone. The more
useful reading is that **the second attempt is usually the first attempt
repeated**. The candidate has no reliable information about *why* they failed —
Texas score reports give limited diagnostic detail — so they re-read the same
material, largely re-studying what they already knew, and walk back in with the
same gaps.

That is a product-shaped hole. A repeat taker is the most motivated buyer in
this market (they have already paid an exam fee and lost a day), they have a
concrete, urgent problem, and **nobody is solving it well.** What they need is
not another course. It is: *here are the four topics that are costing you the
exam, ranked, with the lessons that fix them.*

Your Phase 1 architecture already points this way — topic-tagged questions and
topic-level scoring were in the plan from the start. This data says that is not
a nice-to-have feature, it is the wedge.

## 3. The competitive field

Four rough tiers.

### National platforms — ExamFX, Kaplan Financial Education

The default choices, often bought by employers for new hires rather than by
candidates directly. Broad multi-state, multi-line catalogues; polished; strong
brand trust.

- **ExamFX** markets heavily on pass rate (claims vary by page — 93% and 99%
  both appear) and a "Guarantee Exam": score 80%+ on their readiness exam and
  they refund you if you fail the real one within three days.
- **Kaplan** positions as a guided programme rather than a question bank —
  more structure, live-option add-ons, higher price.

**Where they're weak:** generalists. Texas-specific content (TWIA, FAIR Plan,
coastal windstorm, state forms) is roughly a quarter to a third of the exam and
is where a national vendor's material is thinnest. Access windows are typically
60 days, after which you pay again — painful precisely for the repeat taker.

### Insurance-focused vendors — A.D. Banker, WebCE

Deeper insurance specialism, generally cheaper than the nationals. A.D. Banker
offers classroom-style and live options. WebCE packages Texas P&C with
pre-license IEP credit hours.

### Texas-focused and instructor-led — America's Professor, Test Crushers, All Lines Training

Where the sharpest competition for your positioning sits.

- **America's Professor** — streaming video lectures plus a mailed hardcopy
  textbook.
- **Test Crushers** — video instruction, ~50-day access, unlimited quiz retakes.
- **All Lines Training** — worth studying closely: 100% online, smart quizzes,
  unlimited practice exams, a performance dashboard, exam-readiness reporting,
  and **lifetime access with no completion deadline**. That is the closest thing
  to what you are building, and the lifetime-access model is a direct answer to
  the repeat-taker problem the 60/36 gap exposes.

### Commodity — self-published books, cheap question dumps

Amazon study guides, app-store question banks. Compete on price alone. Not your
market, but they set the floor for what a candidate thinks "some help" costs.

## 4. Indicative pricing — verify before relying on it

From third-party comparisons, **low confidence**:

| Vendor | Reported range | Access window |
| ------ | -------------- | ------------- |
| Kaplan | ~$79 basic → $299+ comprehensive | ~60 days |
| ExamFX | ~$69 → $249 | 60 days, paid extensions |
| A.D. Banker | ~$55 → $120 | 60 days + 30-day extension option |

The **60-day window is near-universal**, and it is a deliberate revenue
mechanism: candidates who fail and need to re-study pay again. It is also the
most obvious thing to compete against, and All Lines Training already is.

## 5. What this means for your product

**Positioning.** Don't be a cheaper ExamFX — you lose that on brand and
catalogue breadth. The defensible position is: *the Texas P&C platform that
tells you exactly what is standing between you and a pass, and proves when you
are ready.* Narrow beats broad when the state-specific quarter of the exam is
where generalists are weakest.

**The wedge is the repeat taker.** Highest motivation, clearest pain, worst
outcomes, weakest current solutions. Worth considering an explicit "failed your
exam?" entry path: a diagnostic assessment that produces a ranked weakness
report and a targeted study plan. That is a genuinely different product from a
course, and it is the one the pass-rate data says is missing.

**Access model.** 60-day windows are the norm and are resented. Lifetime or
until-you-pass access is a real differentiator — and cheap to offer, because
your marginal cost per student is near zero. It also aligns you with the
student's outcome rather than against it.

**On guarantees.** Everyone offers one, so you need one to be credible — but
they are hedged into near-meaninglessness (ExamFX's requires 80% on their own
readiness exam and a retake within three days). Given your topic-level scoring,
you could offer something more honest *and* more defensible: a guarantee tied
to a readiness score your own data shows actually predicts passing. That
requires having the data first, so it is a Phase 4+ decision — but it is worth
not painting yourself out of it now.

### This changes two of the open Phase 2 questions

**Question C — retake policy.** The 60/36 gap argues strongly for unlimited
practice retakes with *varied* question selection. A repeat taker re-answering
the same 50 questions memorises answers rather than learning topics — which is
plausibly part of why repeat pass rates are so poor. Recommend: unlimited
attempts, drawn from the bank so papers differ, with readiness computed from a
recent-weighted average rather than the best-ever score. A best-ever score
rewards a lucky paper, which is exactly the false confidence that sends someone
into a $50 exam unprepared.

**Question D — readiness threshold.** Do not pick a number now. Ship the
readiness *display* without a "you're ready" verdict, collect real
score-versus-outcome data, and set the threshold where your own data says
passing becomes likely. Publishing a threshold you cannot justify is how you end
up with students failing at "90% ready" — worse for them, and worse for you than
having said nothing.

## 6. What I could not verify

- **Exact current pricing.** Sources blocked. Check the four vendors directly.
- **Exam structure.** Sources conflict: one says 150 questions (125 scorable +
  25 pretest), another 145 (100+10 general, 30+5 Texas). **Get the authoritative
  content outline from Pearson VUE** (publication #124401, *Texas Insurance
  Content Outlines*) before building blueprints — this feeds `blueprint_topics`
  directly and being wrong makes every simulation subtly wrong.
- **Published topic weightings.** Approximations only (~70–75% general,
  ~25–30% Texas). Same source needed.
- **Market size.** No figures on annual Texas General Lines candidate volume.
  TDI or NAIC licensing statistics would be the place to look.
- **Competitor conversion, retention or actual pass rates.** Vendors' claims are
  self-reported and unaudited.
